Overview

◔ ~4 min read ✎ intuition-first

An embedding turns a piece of content — a word, a sentence, an image, any chunk of data — into a fixed list of numbers. Train the model right, and things that mean similar things land close together in that list of numbers. Things that mean different things land far apart.

ⓘ

In 30 seconds

Once everything is numbers, "is this like that?" turns into a distance calculation — fast, mechanical, and easy to scale. That one trick powers semantic search, retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), recommendations, clustering, and most of the "the AI understood what I meant" feeling in modern products.

The mental model

Picture a warehouse instead of a library. The librarian doesn't shelve books alphabetically — she places every book at a coordinate in the warehouse. Cookbooks end up in one corner. Murder mysteries end up in another. A book that's half-cookbook, half-memoir floats between the two. To find books "like this one," you don't read titles — you walk a small circle around its spot and grab whatever you bump into.

That warehouse is the embedding space. The coordinates are the vector the list of numbers itself. The librarian is the embedding model — often a transformer like OpenAI's text-embedding-3-small, Cohere's embed models, or an open one like bge or nomic-embed. Feed it text, it hands back coordinates.

Nobody hand-picked what each direction of that space means — the model learned it from billions of examples. "King" sits near "queen"; the trip from "man" to "woman" points roughly the same way as the trip from "king" to "queen." Meaning became geometry. And in real systems that warehouse isn't 2D or 3D — it usually runs 384 to 3072 dimensions the length of the vector, how many numbers it holds wide. Same idea, just far more directions than a person can picture.

What it is not

⚠

Common mix-ups

  • Not a database. Embeddings are the values; a vector database (Pinecone, pgvector, Qdrant) is where you store and search them.
  • Not the same as tokens. Tokens are the chopped-up pieces an LLM reads; embeddings are the meaning-coordinates of those pieces, or of whole texts.
  • Not one-hot encoding or TF-IDF. Those count keywords. Embeddings capture meaning — "automobile" and "car" land next to each other, where keyword counting sees them as unrelated.
  • Not portable across models. A vector from one model means nothing to another. You cannot mix text-embedding-3-small output with bge-large output.

When to reach for it — and when not to

✓ Reach for it when

  • You want search that gets intent, not just keywords — "how do I stop my code from crashing" should find a doc titled "exception handling."
  • You're building RAG and need to pull the right chunks for an LLM.
  • You want to cluster feedback, find near-duplicate content, or recommend "more like this."
  • You want to classify text without training a classifier — just compare against labeled example vectors.

✕ Skip it when

  • You need exact matches, IDs, or structured filters — SQL or a regular index does this better.
  • Your dataset is a few hundred items. A keyword search and a coffee break will do.
  • You need to explain a decision to a regulator. "It was geometrically close" is a hard sell.
  • Your data changes by the second and the cost of re-embedding outweighs the search benefit.

Key vocabulary

Vector / embedding
The list of numbers itself.
Dimension
The length of that list — e.g. 768, 1536, 3072.
Embedding model
The neural net that produces the vector.
Cosine similarity
The usual "how close" measure between two vectors; 1.0 means pointing the same direction.
Vector database
Storage built for nearest-neighbor search over millions of vectors.
ANN (approximate nearest neighbor)
The fast-but-not-perfect search algorithms (HNSW, IVF) that make vector search scale.
Chunking
Splitting long text into small pieces before embedding, so each vector holds one coherent thought.
Semantic search
The flagship use case — retrieve by meaning, not by keyword.
