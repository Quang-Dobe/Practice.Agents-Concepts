Overview

◔ ~3 min read ✎ intuition-first

Every microservice needs the same plumbing: encryption, retries, timeouts, "is that service even up?", request tracing. A service mesh pulls that plumbing out of your app and runs it in the network instead.

ⓘ

In 30 seconds

Split a monolith into 40 microservices and every team starts rebuilding the same networking code, each slightly differently. A mesh moves that code out of the apps and into the infrastructure. Your OrderService still makes a plain call to http://payments; the mesh quietly adds encryption, load balancing, metrics, and a retry on a 503 — no code change, no library upgrade in 40 repos.

The mental model

Picture a city of microservices, each one a building:

  • Without a mesh — every building hires its own security guard, postal worker, translator, and accountant, and they all do the job slightly differently.
  • With a mesh — the city installs a standardized concierge outside every building's front door. Your app only ever talks to its own concierge, in plain language ("send this to payments"); concierges talk to each other in encrypted, logged, retry-capable conversations.

The concierges are the data plane the proxies that sit in the request path and actually move traffic — usually Envoy. Above them sits City Hall, the control plane the piece that never touches a packet, but hands every proxy its rulebook: routing weights, identity checks, fresh certificates. Change a rule in City Hall and every concierge updates within seconds. Dumb-but-fast proxies in the request path, smart-but-out-of-band rule-maker on the side — that split is the architectural heart of every mesh.

The classic deployment is the sidecar a proxy container that runs next to your app in the same pod and shares its network: in Kubernetes your pod runs two containers — your app and an Envoy proxy — and the proxy quietly steals all inbound and outbound traffic. Your app thinks it's calling localhost. The industry is also shifting toward sidecar-less "ambient" modes, where the proxy runs once per node instead of once per pod — same mental model, less overhead. Detail covers that trade-off properly.

What it is not

⚠

Common mix-ups

  • Not an API gateway. A gateway handles north-south traffic (client → cluster); a mesh handles east-west (service → service).
  • Not a load balancer. A mesh does load balance, but that's one feature among many — a plain LB has no identity, policy, or tracing layer.
  • Not a framework or library. The whole point is that it lives outside your code, so polyglot teams get the same features for free.
  • Not Kubernetes itself. Kubernetes gives you basic service discovery; a mesh sits on top and adds the smart networking.

When to reach for it — and when not to

✓ Reach for it when

  • You have 10+ microservices and observability is already a mess of inconsistent logs.
  • You need zero-trust networking — mutual TLS between every service, with rotating certs.
  • You want progressive delivery: canary releases, traffic shifting by header, fault injection in staging.
  • Compliance demands every internal call be encrypted and audited, and you can't patch 40 services to do it.

✕ Skip it when

  • You have 3 services. The mesh is more moving parts than the system it manages.
  • Latency is sacred (HFT, real-time gaming) — every hop through a proxy costs microseconds.
  • No one on the team owns Kubernetes or the control plane. A mesh's failure modes are not friendly to part-timers.
  • A single-language framework already gives you most of this (e.g. Spring Cloud) and you aren't polyglot.

Key vocabulary

Data plane
The proxies in the request path.
Control plane
The brain that configures the proxies.
Sidecar
A proxy container running next to your app in the same pod.
Envoy
The C++ proxy most meshes use as their data plane.
mTLS
Mutual TLS — both sides prove identity with certificates.
Ambient mode
Sidecar-less mesh; proxies run per node, not per pod.
Istio / Linkerd / Cilium
The three meshes you'll hear about most.
Waypoint proxy
An optional L7 proxy used in ambient mode for richer policy.
East-west traffic
Service-to-service traffic — the mesh's home turf.
Ingress / Egress gateway
Mesh-managed edge points for traffic entering or leaving the cluster.
