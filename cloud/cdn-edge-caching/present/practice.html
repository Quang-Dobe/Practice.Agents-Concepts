Practice — how it plays out for real

● ~11 min read ✎ best practices · anti-patterns · checklist

Where you'll meet it

The CDN is the layer you forget exists until something breaks. It sits in front of your marketing site, your SPA's static bundle, your image uploads, your unauthenticated API endpoints, and increasingly your server-rendered HTML. Your framework's default Cache-Control headers already do something — the job in production is making sure they do the right thing.

E-commerce

Load-bearing: product pages, category listings, image variants. A botched purge during a price change becomes a support incident within minutes.

Media and news

Micro-caching turns a single-origin Rails or WordPress site into one that survives the front page of Hacker News.

B2B platforms, global users

Keeps p99 latency from being dominated by transatlantic round trips to one us-east-1 deployment.

Inherited setups

You'll often find a half-configured CDN — enabled years ago, hit ratio stuck at 42%, nobody knows why. The skill is reading the headers and the analytics to find the cache-killer.

Best practices

1

Fingerprint your static assets and cache them forever

Do

Build pipelines (Vite, webpack, esbuild) should emit content-hashed filenames (app.4f2a1b.js). Serve those with Cache-Control: public, max-age=31536000, immutable.

Why

Invalidation is the hardest part of caching. Fingerprinting deletes the problem — old URLs age out, new URLs are fresh by definition. immutable also stops browsers revalidating on every reload.

Avoid

Serving app.js with a short TTL and purging on every deploy — you pay a global MISS storm every release.

2

Split browser TTL from edge TTL with s-maxage

Do

For HTML and JSON, use Cache-Control: public, max-age=0, s-maxage=300, stale-while-revalidate=60. Browsers always revalidate; the edge holds the response for 5 minutes.

Why

You cannot purge a browser cache. Keeping the user-side TTL low means a purge actually fixes what the user sees; the edge still absorbs ~99% of origin load on hot paths.

Avoid

A single max-age=3600 shared between both. After a purge, users still see stale content for up to an hour.

3

Use surrogate keys / cache tags for granular invalidation

Do

Tag responses by domain entity. A product page emits Surrogate-Key: product-42 category-shoes brand-acme; a price update fires one purge for product-42. Cloudflare Enterprise calls this Cache-Tag.

Why

Without tags your only tools are "purge by URL" (enumerate every variant) or "purge everything" (cause an origin stampede). Tags invalidate exactly what a mutation touched.

Avoid

A wildcard purge after every CMS edit — works small, silently becomes a self-inflicted DDoS as you grow.

4

Prefer soft purge plus stale-while-revalidate

Do

When you purge, soft-purge (mark stale) rather than hard-purge (delete). Pair with stale-while-revalidate=60 and stale-if-error=86400.

Why

A hard purge guarantees a MISS on the next request, and N concurrent MISSes hit origin at once. Soft purge serves stale for milliseconds while one fetch refills the entry; stale-if-error keeps the site up through a multi-hour origin outage on warm paths.

Avoid

Hard-purging hot paths during traffic spikes — exactly when you can't afford the origin RPS.

5

Enable origin shielding / tiered cache

Do

Turn on Cloudflare's Smart Tiered Cache, CloudFront's Origin Shield, or Fastly's Shielding. Pick the shield POP closest to your origin region.

Why

Without it, every one of roughly 300 edge POPs that gets a MISS goes straight to origin. With it, MISSes funnel through a handful of upper-tier POPs where coalescing works globally. Real-world origin RPS often drops 5–10× after enabling it.

Avoid

Skipping it because "we already have a CDN." A flat-topology CDN can still hammer origin during a viral moment.

6

Strip cookies and tracking params from the cache key

Do

Configure the CDN to ignore utm_*, fbclid, gclid, and analytics cookies on cacheable paths. Use Cloudflare's Query String Sort or "ignore query string" where order doesn't matter.

Why

Every distinct query string or cookie value is a separate cache entry. Five UTM variants on one campaign multiply the key space by five. Session cookies in the key collapse hit ratio to near zero.

Avoid

Letting the default "include full URL and all cookies" key persist unaudited.

7

Normalize Vary axes and never use Vary: Cookie

Do

Emit only Vary: Accept-Encoding and, if needed, a CDN-provided normalized header (CF-Device-Type, CloudFront-Is-Mobile-Viewer). Strip raw User-Agent and Cookie from any Vary.

Why

Vary: User-Agent produces thousands of variants. Vary: Cookie is the same disaster as keying on cookies — either silently destroys hit ratio.

Avoid

Letting a backend framework auto-emit Vary: Cookie because it sets a session cookie. Strip the cookie on cacheable routes before the response leaves origin.

8

Cache by intent, not by extension

Do

Decide caching policy per route in code, with explicit Cache-Control headers, and a matching CDN rule. Static assets, public HTML, authenticated JSON, and personalized HTML each get a documented policy.

Why

"Cache everything that looks like an image" rules drift. A PDF-generation endpoint ending in .pdf shouldn't be cached, but a path-suffix rule will cache it anyway.

Avoid

Configuring caching only in the dashboard — the next engineer has no idea the rule exists until something breaks.

9

Defend against cache poisoning from unkeyed inputs

Do

Identify every header your origin reflects into a response (X-Forwarded-Host, X-Original-URL, custom X-*). Either strip them at the edge, include them in the cache key, or never reflect them.

Why

An attacker who finds an unkeyed header that changes the response can poison one cache entry that then serves their payload to every user routed to that POP.

Avoid

Trusting Host-like headers to build absolute URLs. If origin emits <link href="https://{Host}/style.css">, someone will set Host: evil.com.

10

Personalize without breaking the cache

Do

Push personalization out of the document. Render the shell at the edge, hydrate per-user data with a separate uncached fetch, or use ESI / edge functions to assemble fragments with independent TTLs. For binary splits (logged-in vs. anonymous, country, plan tier), compute a coarse variant key — not one per user.

Why

Personalization is the most common reason teams declare a route "uncacheable," but almost always the cacheable 90% can be separated from the personalized 10%. Caching the shell can turn p95 from 800 ms to 40 ms even when the user-specific bit still hits origin.

Avoid

Stuffing the username into the HTML and concluding the page can't be cached — inject it client-side or via an edge worker instead.

11

Monitor hit ratio per surface, not site-wide

Do

Track hit ratio bucketed by content type and route (/_next/static/*, /api/products/*, /). Alert when a specific bucket regresses, not when the global number wobbles. Watch origin egress, cache-key cardinality, and purge-API latency.

Why

A site-wide 85% hit ratio can hide a freshly-deployed route sitting at 4%. Key cardinality how many distinct cache-key values a route can produce silently explodes when someone adds a new query param to product links.

Avoid

Watching only the single "Cache Hit Ratio" number on the vendor dashboard — it averages your wins with your losses.

Anti-patterns to recognize

✕

no-cache as a panic button

Slapping Cache-Control: no-cache, no-store on every endpoint because one bug leaked private data. Now everything serves from origin and latency and bill both double. Fix the specific route, don't disable the layer.

✕

Purge-on-every-deploy

CI runs a global purge after each release. Fine at 100 RPS, causes a coordinated MISS storm at 10k RPS. Fingerprint assets and tag dynamic content instead.

✕

Cookies in the cache key by default

Vendor or framework defaults to "include all cookies"; hit ratio is mysteriously 30%. Strip session cookies on cacheable routes; whitelist only cookies that actually change rendering.

✕

Query-string sprawl

Marketing appends ?utm_source=... to every shared link; the CDN keys on the full query and the same article becomes 50 cache entries. Strip tracking params at the edge.

✕

Set-Cookie leaking through cacheable responses

A login handler accidentally emits Set-Cookie on a cached path; some CDNs cache and serve that cookie to other users — a session-hijack vector. Strip Set-Cookie on cacheable responses, hard.

✕

Trusting heuristic caching

Origin sends no Cache-Control; the CDN applies a heuristic TTL (often 10% of Last-Modified age) and caches something never meant to be cached. Always send explicit headers from origin.

✕

Treating the CDN as a free database

Storing API responses with 24-hour TTLs and using purges as writes. Works until purge latency, eventual consistency, and key cardinality bite at once. If you need a key-value store, use one.

✕

Web cache deception an attacker tricks a cache into storing a private response by disguising the URL as a static asset

An attacker requests /profile/account.css — the framework returns the user's profile, the CDN sees a .css extension and caches it, and the attacker fetches it from another session. Match cache rules to content-type, not URL suffix.

Real-world usage patterns

News site under a traffic spike

A regional newspaper's story trends nationally. Origin is a single Rails monolith. Fastly runs micro-caching at a 10-second TTL on article HTML, surrogate keys per article ID, and stale-while-revalidate=60. Traffic jumps from 200 to 80k RPS but lands on origin as ~6 RPS thanks to coalescing and tiered cache.

E-commerce product catalog

A mid-size retailer tags product pages with Surrogate-Key: product-{id} category-{slug}. Inventory updates fire one tag purge per affected SKU. Soft purge plus stale-if-error=3600 means a midnight origin deploy doesn't take the store down.

SaaS dashboard, global users

An authenticated dashboard is mostly uncacheable, so the team splits the shell HTML (edge-cached, 5-minute TTL, anonymous variant only) from /api/me (never cached). An edge worker injects the display name client-side. P95 page load drops from 1.2 s to 180 ms in APAC.

Public API with API keys

A developer API serves the same data to many clients with per-key rate limits. The cache key includes only the path and sorted query; the API-key header is unkeyed and used only for auth/rate-limit at the edge. Hit ratio is above 95%, origin sees ~3% of public traffic.

Video segment delivery

A streaming service serves HLS segments (segment-00042.ts) with year-long TTLs and fingerprinted manifests. Origin shielding via CloudFront Origin Shield cuts origin egress by ~95%.

Operational checklist

✓

Before you call it done

  • Hit ratio per route bucket is monitored, with alerts on regressions per bucket, not just the global average.
  • Cache-key cardinality is tracked or audited; new query params and headers in the key trigger a review.
  • Origin egress and origin RPS are graphed alongside CDN traffic — a divergence is the first signal of a cache-killer deploy.
  • Purge propagation latency has been measured under load, not just read off the vendor's marketing page.
  • Set-Cookie on cacheable paths is impossible by construction (middleware strips it) or asserted in tests.
  • Reflected headers (Host, X-Forwarded-*, custom) are either stripped at the edge or included in the key — write down which.
  • Stale-if-error is configured on critical paths; a contrived origin outage in staging confirms the site still serves.
  • Onboarding doc explains: how to set cache headers, how to issue a purge, who owns surrogate-key tagging, and the Slack channel that pages when hit ratio drops.
  • Cost model: the team knows the per-GB egress price, the purge API rate limits, and which features add a line item.
  • Security review has confirmed no cacheable route returns user-private data — tested by requesting the same URL with two sessions and diffing.

How it evolves in a codebase

  1. Static assets only. A CDN goes in front of static assets and the team calls it done. Hit ratio is fine because the framework already fingerprints JS/CSS.
  2. HTML enters the picture. Someone wants to cache HTML too — usually because TTFB in a far region is embarrassing. That's when the team meets Vary, session cookies, and the first cache-key foot-gun.
  3. URL purges to tag-based purges. Teams put this off because tagging means touching every controller. They do it the day a routine CMS edit triggers a 30-second origin overload, or a security review flags a stale price shown after an inventory update. Surrogate keys force a design decision: which entities own which cached views — hard to add later, easy to bake in early.
  4. The edge becomes programmable. At scale, edge workers compute custom cache keys, ESI assembles personalized pages from cached fragments, and "origin" increasingly means a write path plus cold-storage backup. The instinct shifts from "what can I cache?" to "what specifically can I not cache, and why?"

Further reading

  • RFC 9111: HTTP Caching — the spec. Read at least sections 4 (constructing responses from caches) and 5 (header definitions); everything vendors do is a flavor of this.
  • PortSwigger: Web Cache Poisoning — James Kettle's research. Will make you paranoid in a productive way.
  • Fastly: Working with surrogate keys — the canonical pattern for granular invalidation, even off Fastly.
  • Cloudflare: Tiered Cache — a readable explanation of origin shielding mechanics that applies to every CDN.
  • Fastly blog: stale-while-revalidate — the directive that quietly does most of the resilience work in a production cache setup.
  • Google Web Fundamentals: HTTP caching — Jake Archibald's pragmatic guide; the "immutable fingerprinted vs. always-revalidated" framing is the right mental model for most teams.
