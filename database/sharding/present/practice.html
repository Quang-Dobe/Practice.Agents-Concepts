Practice — how it plays out for real

● ~10 min read ✎ best practices · anti-patterns · checklist

Where you'll meet it

In a typical multi-tenant SaaS backend, sharding sits between the API tier and the relational database once one primary stops absorbing the write rate: a router (Vitess vtgate, MongoDB mongos, a Citus coordinator, or a homemade proxy) in front of N MySQL/Postgres clusters, each replicated for its own high availability. You'll run into it in four settings:

Multi-tenant SaaS

A routing component in front of N clusters. Same shape everywhere: Vitess, MongoDB, Citus, or a homemade proxy like Figma's DBProxy.

Consumer social feed

The reason user_id = ? stays under 10 ms on a 50-billion-row table. Pick the wrong key and the next viral post takes a shard down.

Serverless database

DynamoDB, Cosmos DB, Aurora DSQL, Spanner — you don't operate sharding, but partition keys and per-partition limits are still yours to design.

Regulated workload

EU vs US residency, healthcare per-region rules. The shard key pins a customer's data to a physical region for compliance, not performance.

Best practices

1

Choose the shard key from the dominant query, not the data model

Do

Pick the key that appears in the WHERE clause of over 95% of your hot-path queries — in multi-tenant SaaS, almost always tenant_id. Verify with real query logs, not intuition.

Why

Any read missing the shard key triggers a scatter-gather across every shard. Tail latency rises with shard count, and one slow shard sets p99 for everyone.

Avoid

Sharding by created_at or id because it "feels even" — that breaks every per-tenant query.

2

Hash, don't range, unless you have a concrete reason

Do

Default to hash sharding, or consistent hashing with virtual nodes. Reach for range sharding only when range scans dominate — time-series, append-only logs with TTL-based deletion — and you have a plan for the tail-end hotspot.

Why

Range sharding plus a monotonic key — an auto-increment ID, now() — sends every new write to the rightmost shard. You scaled horizontally and bought a single-writer system.

Avoid

Range sharding on timestamp for a high-write event stream with no bucketing or salting.

3

Pick a key that is immutable, high-cardinality, and well-distributed

Do

The key must never change for a row, must have far more distinct values than you'll ever have shards, and should have access skew under roughly 4x between the hottest and median value.

Why

A mutable key means moving a row across shards on every update — most engines forbid it outright. A low-cardinality key (country_code, plan_tier) creates jumbo chunks in MongoDB or ranges the balancer can't split in Vitess.

Avoid

status, region, is_active, or anything Boolean-ish.

4

Co-locate related entities under the same shard key

Do

Shard users, orders, invoices, and audit_log all by the same customer_id — Citus's distribution-column alignment, Vitess's keyspace IDs, DynamoDB's shared partition key.

Why

Local joins and single-shard transactions stay cheap. The moment a parent and its children live on different shards, every join becomes scatter-gather and every write becomes a two-phase-commit candidate.

Avoid

Sharding orders by order_id while users is sharded by user_id — "show me this user's orders" now touches every shard.

5

Treat cross-shard transactions as a code smell, not a feature

Do

Design the data model so multi-row writes stay on one shard. When truly unavoidable, use idempotent, retry-safe operations with a saga or outbox pattern; reserve two-phase commit for the few flows that genuinely need atomicity.

Why

Cross-shard 2PC commit latency is bounded by the slowest participant — typically 2–10x a single-shard write. A coordinator crash mid-prepare stalls every participant until manual cleanup.

Avoid

Turning on two-phase commit globally because "transactions should just work."

6

Build the routing layer before you need a second shard

Do

Even on a single physical database, add a routing abstraction and write every query as if it were sharded — always carrying the shard key. Figma did exactly this with logical "sharded views" on one Postgres instance before any physical split.

Why

Adding a shard key to 1,400 call sites under incident pressure is the worst possible time to do it. Doing it cold, as feature work, takes a fraction of the effort.

Avoid

"We'll add the routing layer when we shard." You won't — you'll be too busy firefighting.

7

Monitor per-shard metrics, not just cluster aggregates

Do

Dashboards must show CPU, IOPS, replication lag, p99 latency, QPS, and storage broken down by shard. Alert on any shard exceeding 2x the median before alerting on cluster totals.

Why

A hot shard at 95% CPU averages out to a healthy-looking 40% across an 8-shard cluster. Dashboards look fine right up until the hot shard tips over.

Avoid

Reusing a single-node dashboard and adding sum() everywhere.

8

Practice resharding before you need it

Do

Run a resharding drill in staging on realistic data volumes at least once a quarter. Measure how long copy, catch-up, and cutover actually take for your largest shard, and kill the source primary mid-copy.

Why

Resharding under real pressure — a hot shard degrading production right now — is when teams discover their bulk-copy can't keep up with the live write rate, the resharding lag spiral from Detail.

Avoid

Assuming the vendor docs' "few seconds of read-only cutover" applies to your write rate without measuring it.

9

Prefer vendor-managed sharding unless you have an SRE team

Do

For most teams under roughly 5 engineers on data infrastructure, use Vitess (PlanetScale), Citus (Azure Cosmos DB for PostgreSQL), MongoDB Atlas sharded clusters, or DynamoDB/Spanner.

Why

Online DDL, resharding, topology management, and shard-aware connection pooling are each multi-quarter projects. Vendors have already solved them; a small team building it in-house stalls at "barely works."

Avoid

Rebuilding ProxySQL with custom routing rules because "it's just a hash function."

10

Plan for schema migrations across N shards from day one

Do

Use a tool that applies DDL to every shard atomically with rollback — Vitess OnlineDDL, gh-ost coordinated across shards, Citus's coordinator-driven DDL — and verify schema parity in CI.

Why

A migration that succeeds on 7 of 8 shards leaves one shard with a missing column. Every query touching it fails for that slice of users — a partial outage that's hard to diagnose.

Avoid

A bash loop that runs ALTER TABLE against each shard and hopes for the best.

Anti-patterns to recognize

✕

Sharding too early

Splitting a 40 GB database into 8 shards "to be ready." You inherit rebalancing, cross-shard joins, and distributed schema migrations without the throughput need — a bigger box would have been cheaper for two years. Fix indexes and add read replicas first.

✕

Auto-increment ID with range partitioning

Every new row lands on the last shard, so writes don't scale and old shards go cold. Use hash sharding, or shard by a tenant key.

✕

The "one tenant to rule them all" shard key

Sharding by customer_id when one customer holds 60% of the data. The whale tenant saturates one shard. Split that tenant across sub-keys, or move it to a dedicated shard via a directory map.

✕

Mutable shard key

Sharding users by their current region, then letting users change region. The row now belongs on a different shard but physically sits on the old one — queries silently return empty results. Use an immutable surrogate or a directory layer.

✕

Trusting adaptive capacity to save a bad partition key

"Adaptive capacity will handle it" — until a burst exceeds the per-partition 1,000 WCU ceiling for 5+ minutes before split-for-heat kicks in. Design for even distribution; adaptive capacity is a safety net, not a strategy.

✕

Cross-shard query creep

A small admin screen adds "show me all pending orders." It looks fine in dev with 3 shards; in production it scatters to 64 shards on every page load and tail latency triples. Gate non-shard-key queries behind explicit review.

✕

Treating reference data like sharded data

Routing countries, currencies, or feature_flags through the shard path. Replicate them to every shard as reference tables (Citus) or lookup vindexes (Vitess) so joins stay local.

✕

A routing layer with no cache, or an unbounded cache TTL

No cache turns the topology store into the hot path. A forever-cached topology sends clients to a shard that's already retired. Cache the shard map with a 1–10 second TTL and push-based invalidation.

Real-world usage patterns

Multi-tenant B2B SaaS, 20k tenants

Hash-sharded by tenant_id across 16 MySQL shards behind Vitess. About 98% of queries carry tenant_id; the rest run against a separate CDC-fed analytics replica. Lesson: that analytics escape valve is what keeps the OLTP path clean.

Consumer social feed, 50M MAU

Sharded by user_id with consistent hashing; per-user data stays single-shard. Lesson: the team deliberately did not shard posts by post_id — "user plus their posts" is the dominant query, so posts stay on the author's shard.

Time-series telemetry, 200k writes/sec

Range-sharded by (device_id_hash, hour_bucket). The hash prevents a rightmost-shard hotspot; the hour bucket lets old data be deleted by dropping whole shards. Lesson: dropping a shard for TTL is orders of magnitude cheaper than DELETE WHERE timestamp < ....

DynamoDB-backed event store

Partition key is tenant_id#YYYYMMDD, salted with the date to spread one tenant's writes across partitions. Lesson: added only after one large tenant hit the per-partition WCU cap — they wish they'd built it in from day one.

Figma-style pre-sharding

Logical shards as Postgres views, routed through DBProxy, with the physical split deferred. Lesson: the interface — every query carries a shard key, every transaction stays single-shard — is what made the eventual physical migration take days instead of months.

Operational checklist

✓

Before you call it done

  • Monitoring: per-shard CPU, QPS, p99 latency, replication lag, and storage on the dashboard, with an alert when any shard exceeds 2x the median?
  • Hot-key detection: a job sampling the top-100 shard-key values by traffic every 5 minutes, so a celebrity hot key is caught within the hour?
  • Resharding: a full drill run in staging in the last 90 days, including a forced primary kill mid-copy?
  • Schema migrations: DDL applied to all shards atomically with rollback, and CI checking for schema drift?
  • Cross-shard queries: a lint or proxy rule flagging queries without the shard key, and a budget on scatter-gather queries?
  • Failure handling: documented, tested behavior when one shard's primary is unreachable for 60 seconds?
  • Security: per-shard credentials, rotated independently, with no app instance able to read beyond its tenant scope?
  • Cost: per-shard and per-tenant cost visible, so a whale tenant shows up before the bill does?
  • Backups: cross-shard, point-in-time consistent snapshots, not just independent per-shard ones?
  • Onboarding: can a new engineer name the shard key, the routing layer, and the resharding tool on day one?

How it evolves in a codebase

  1. No sharding. One Postgres or MySQL primary and a few read replicas. Scaling moves are vertical first, then more replicas, then a cache in front of hot reads, then maybe pulling one heavy table onto its own database. None of this is sharding yet.
  2. First physical split. Write throughput on the primary, or a working set that no longer fits in RAM, finally forces it. Hundreds of existing queries assume a single database; many don't carry a shard key, some join across what will become shard boundaries.
  3. Figma-style de-risking. The modern move: introduce a routing proxy and logical sharding before any physical split. Teams that skip this spend 6–18 months half-migrated, with elaborate conditional routing.
  4. Maturity. Convergence on a vendor-managed control plane (Vitess, Citus, DynamoDB), strict lint rules on shard-key predicates, a separate analytics pipeline for non-shard-key queries, and per-tenant cost dashboards.
  5. Multi-region. The next evolution is usually multi-region sharding for residency and latency — which reopens most of these decisions, because the routing function now also encodes geography.
