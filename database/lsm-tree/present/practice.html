Practice — how it plays out for real

● ~9 min read ✎ best practices · anti-patterns · checklist

Where you'll meet it

You rarely "use an LSM tree" on purpose — you inherit one. The moment your team picks Cassandra, ScyllaDB, HBase, or any RocksDB-backed system, every quirk below becomes your problem. The LSM tree is the silent layer between the query engine and the block device, and it leaks its trade-offs upward as latency spikes, disk-full pages, and CPU you can't explain.

Metrics / observability

Prometheus-style TSDBs and InfluxDB ingest a firehose of points. The LSM-like engine is why you can take millions of writes per second on commodity SSDs — and why "disk at 95%, compaction backlog growing" pages you at 3 a.m.

Wide-column stores

Cassandra and ScyllaDB run the LSM/SSTable model directly. Your read p99, write-stall incidents, and storage bill all trace back to memtable, compaction, and Bloom filter tuning.

NewSQL on RocksDB

CockroachDB, TiKV, YugabyteDB, and MyRocks put a RocksDB instance under each shard. Most teams never touch its settings until something breaks.

Stream processors

Kafka Streams and Flink keep windowed aggregation state in an embedded RocksDB, right next to the hot path.

The recurring shape: writes are cheap and you fall in love with the system, then weeks later compaction, tombstones, or space amplification present a bill you didn't budget for.

Best practices

1

Match the compaction strategy to the read/write/delete shape, not the default

Do

Use leveled compaction for read-heavy and update-heavy tables; size-tiered for write-heavy, append-mostly tables; time-windowed for TTL'd time-series.

Why

The strategy is the read-vs-write-vs-space amplification dial. Get it wrong and you either get a read p99 that fans out across dozens of SSTables, or write amplification north of 10x burning your SSD's endurance.

Avoid

Leaving Cassandra on the size-tiered default for a heavily updated, read-latency-sensitive table — reads end up scanning many overlapping tiers.

2

Use time-windowed compaction for anything with a uniform TTL, and only that

Do

Pick the compaction window size so the TTL spans roughly 20–30 windows — a 90-day TTL suggests ~3-day windows. Whole expired SSTables get dropped without rewriting data.

Why

TWCS time-windowed compaction strategy — groups data by write time so a whole expired file can be dropped reclaims space by deleting files, not by merging tombstones — the cheapest possible reclaim. Size-tiered or leveled compaction would rewrite expiring data repeatedly instead.

Avoid

Using it on a table that also does explicit deletes of non-TTL'd rows — tombstones and the data they shadow land in different time windows and never compact together.

3

Size memtables and compaction parallelism so compaction keeps up with ingest

Do

Watch the L0 file count and pending-compaction bytes. If they climb under steady load, raise the number of background compaction jobs and the number of memtables allowed to absorb bursts.

Why

When L0 overflows faster than background workers drain it, the engine throttles then stops writers — a latency cliff, not a clean error.

Avoid

Running a single compaction thread on a multi-core box with SSDs that can absorb far more concurrent I/O.

4

Size Bloom filters at ~10 bits per key and keep them resident

Do

Default to 10 bits per key (~1% false-positive rate). Enable whole-key filtering for point gets; add a prefix extractor only if you do prefix range scans. Consider the Ribbon filter for ~30% less filter memory at the same false-positive rate when CPU is cheaper than RAM.

Why

Below ~5 bits per key, false positives turn negative lookups into wasted block reads — read amplification you'll see as elevated p99 on keys that don't even exist.

Avoid

Disabling filters "to save memory," then wondering why every miss hits disk.

5

Provision disk for transient space amplification, not just logical size

Do

Budget roughly 2x logical size for size-tiered compaction — a large tier merge needs room for the inputs plus the output at the same time. Leveled compaction is tighter (~1.1x) but spikier under backlog.

Why

Compaction writes the merged output before deleting the inputs. Run a disk too close to full and one big compaction wedges the node — it can neither compact nor free space. That's space amplification extra disk space consumed by stale copies and in-flight merges beyond the logical data size biting you at the worst time.

Avoid

Running a manual full compaction on a size-tiered table in production — it fuses everything into one giant SSTable with no same-size peer left to merge with.

6

Treat deletes as expensive writes

Do

Prefer TTLs over explicit deletes; model data so you delete whole partitions or SSTables rather than scattered cells. Tune gc_grace_seconds deliberately — Cassandra's 10-day default exists for tombstone propagation across replicas, not performance.

Why

A tombstone is a live row until both it and every shadowed copy are compacted together and the grace period passes. Until then, reads and range scans must read past every tombstone.

Avoid

Queue-on-Cassandra patterns — insert, read, delete — that manufacture tombstone storms and eventually fail reads.

7

Never range-scan across a wall of tombstones

Do

Keep the tombstone-warn threshold (1000) and tombstone-failure threshold (100,000) as guardrails, and design partitions so a scan never crosses thousands of deleted cells.

Why

Tombstones can't be paged — the coordinator must hold all encountered ones in memory, since other replicas may not know about the delete yet. Latency and heap grow with tombstone count until the query trips the failure threshold.

Avoid

Reading "all undeleted rows in a partition" that's mostly deletes — that scan reads every tombstone first.

8

Tune write-stall thresholds intentionally and alert before them

Do

Set the soft and hard pending-compaction-bytes limits knowing the soft limit slows writers and the hard limit stops them. Alert on the soft limit so you act before the hard one.

Why

Hitting the hard limit is a full write stall — to the application it looks like the database hung. The soft limit is your early warning.

Avoid

Discovering these knobs exist only while reading the log file during the incident.

9

Reduce write amplification before adding hardware

Do

Anything that lowers write amplification — larger memtables, fewer levels, better compression, a right-sized level multiplier (RocksDB default 10) — speeds up compaction, because compaction is the bytes being rewritten.

Why

A compaction backlog is usually a write-amplification problem wearing an I/O-saturation costume; adding disks without fixing amplification just delays the next stall.

Avoid

Scaling out the cluster to mask a single-node tuning problem.

Anti-patterns to recognize

✕

LSM as a queue or work queue

Insert task, poll, delete done-task. It looks like a table but generates a tombstone per delete; reads slow to a crawl and eventually fail at the tombstone threshold. Use an actual queue — Kafka, SQS, Redis streams.

✕

Read-heavy point lookups on a cold working set

Choosing an LSM store for a key-value cache whose working set exceeds RAM. Each miss fans out across SSTables where a B-tree would do one seek. Use a B-tree engine or an actual cache.

✕

Manual major compaction "to clean things up"

A full compaction on a size-tiered table merges everything into one monster SSTable that never compacts again, so space stops recovering normally. Let the strategy do its job, or switch to leveled/time-windowed first.

✕

Disabling Bloom filters to save RAM

Saves a little memory, then turns every nonexistent-key lookup into disk I/O. Keep filters; shrink bits per key or switch to Ribbon if memory is truly tight.

✕

Many different TTLs on one time-windowed table

Breaks the "drop the whole expired SSTable" optimization, because windows never fully expire. Use a single table-level TTL.

✕

Ignoring space amplification until the disk fills

Logical data is 400 GB, you provisioned 500 GB, then a tier merge needs another 400 GB transiently and the node wedges. Size for the strategy's worst-case amplification.

✕

Treating compaction CPU as a leak

Seeing constant background CPU/I/O and throttling compaction to "fix" it — which just moves the cost to read amplification and a future write stall.

Real-world usage patterns

Metrics platform, tens of millions of points/sec

A TSDB ingesting from thousands of agents stores each metric with a fixed retention TTL, using time-windowed compaction so a whole window's data expires and its files are deleted as a unit. Lesson: retention changes are nearly free — drop old windows — but re-tuning the window size afterward is painful, since windows already on disk keep their old boundaries.

Wide-column store behind a social feed

A Cassandra/Scylla cluster holds per-user timelines, append-heavy with occasional edits, on size-tiered compaction. Read p99 quietly degraded over months as overlapping tiers piled up. Lesson: the fix was moving read-hot tables to leveled compaction, not adding nodes — amplification trade-offs drift with data volume.

NewSQL OLTP on RocksDB

A distributed SQL database puts a RocksDB/Pebble instance under each range. A batch import saturated compaction on a few hot ranges, triggering write stalls the app saw as transaction timeouts. Lesson: in a sharded LSM system you don't have one compaction problem, you have N — per-shard metrics, not cluster averages, find it.

Kafka Streams / Flink state store

A stream processor keeps windowed aggregation state in an embedded RocksDB. Under high cardinality, compaction competed with the processing threads for disk and CPU, raising end-to-end lag. Lesson: when the LSM sits next to compute, compaction is not "background" — cap its resource use explicitly.

Operational checklist

✓

Before you call it done

  • Monitoring: are L0 file count, pending-compaction bytes, compaction throughput vs. ingest rate, and read/write/space amplification tracked per shard, not just cluster-wide?
  • Write stalls: do you alert on the soft pending-compaction limit before the hard limit is hit?
  • Tombstones: for delete/TTL tables, is tombstone-per-read count monitored and well under the failure threshold? Is gc_grace_seconds set deliberately?
  • Disk headroom: is free space sized for the chosen strategy's transient amplification (~2x logical for size-tiered) so a big compaction can't wedge the node?
  • Failure handling: have you tested what happens when ingest outpaces compaction for an hour?
  • Bloom filters: are filters enabled, sized ~10 bits per key, and resident in memory for open files?
  • Range scans: have you load-tested range scans — which can't use Bloom filters — separately from point reads?
  • Cost: do you know your write amplification factor? It drives SSD wear and your cloud IOPS/throughput bill directly.
  • Onboarding: can a new on-call engineer find the compaction-stats command and read the L0/backlog signals on day one?

How it evolves in a codebase

  1. Honeymoon. Teams accept the defaults. Ingestion is smooth, writes are cheap, and the LSM tree is invisible. This lasts as long as the data stays small relative to RAM and disk — nobody tunes compaction because nothing is broken yet.
  2. First painful migration. At scale, read p99 creeps up (overlapping SSTables under size-tiered), or the disk fills faster than logical growth explains (space amplification and tombstones), or ingest bursts trigger write stalls that look like outages. Teams discover compaction strategies exist and often switch strategy on a live table — an expensive, I/O-heavy rewrite scheduled for off-peak. Delete-heavy workloads hit the tombstone wall around the same time.
  3. Mature usage. Amplification becomes a first-class, monitored budget: per-table compaction strategies chosen on purpose, alerts on backlog and stalls, disk provisioned for worst-case amplification, and compaction CPU/I/O treated as a permanent line item, not a bug.
