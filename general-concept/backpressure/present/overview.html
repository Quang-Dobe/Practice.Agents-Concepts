Overview

◔ ~3 min read ✎ intuition-first

A producer makes data and a consumer processes it. They almost never run at the same speed. Backpressure is the family of techniques that handle that mismatch on purpose, instead of letting memory balloon, latency spike, or the whole process crash.

ⓘ

In 30 seconds

This shows up everywhere: network sockets, message queues, streams, UI event loops. If you've ever watched a Kafka consumer fall hours behind, or seen a Node.js stream eat all your RAM, you've met the absence of backpressure.

The mental model

Picture a busy restaurant kitchen on a Friday night. Waiters (the producers whatever makes the data) keep firing tickets at the line cook (the consumer whatever processes the data). The cook can only plate so many dishes a minute. When tickets arrive faster than plates leave, the kitchen has exactly four moves — and they are the same four moves every backpressure-aware system has:

  • Buffer. Clip the tickets to a rail. Works until the rail's high-water mark is full. Then what?
  • Drop. Toss the new tickets in the bin. The customer never eats, but the kitchen survives.
  • Block. Tell the waiters to stop taking orders until the cook catches up. The dining room queues at the door.
  • Signal slowdown. Wave the waiters off: bring three tickets at a time, not thirty. The pace adjusts right at the source.

That fourth option is reactive backpressure the consumer states its real appetite, and the producer respects it. The first three happen whether or not anyone designed for them, and usually in their worst form: silent drops, out-of-memory kills, latency nobody can explain.

What it is not

⚠

Common mix-ups

  • Not rate limiting. Rate limiting caps a producer from the outside ("max 100 req/sec"). Backpressure is the consumer's own voice from the inside ("I can take 7 more right now").
  • Not load shedding. Load shedding is one specific response — drop — taken under stress. Backpressure is the broader conversation that decides whether to shed at all.
  • Not a queue. A queue is just the buffer option. Backpressure is what you do when that queue fills up.
  • Not retry logic. A retry pushes work back into the system. Backpressure tries to stop pushing so much in, in the first place.

When to reach for it — and when not to

✓ Reach for it when

  • You stream data between services and the consumer's speed varies (DB writers, downstream APIs, batch ML inference).
  • You read large files or network responses chunk-by-chunk on a constrained worker.
  • You build a message-driven system where one slow subscriber must not poison the whole topic.
  • A producer is "fire and forget" but the consumer cannot be.

✕ Skip it when

  • It's request/response and the client already waits synchronously — TCP and the call stack give you this for free.
  • It's a tiny in-memory pipeline where producer and consumer demonstrably run at the same speed.
  • It's a one-shot batch job that finishes in seconds; the engineering cost outweighs the risk.

Key vocabulary

Producer / Publisher
The source of data.
Consumer / Subscriber
The sink that processes it.
Buffer
Memory that holds in-flight items between the two.
Demand
How many items the consumer is currently willing to accept.
Drop / Shed
Discard items the system cannot handle.
Block
Pause the producer until capacity frees up.
Lag
The gap between produced and consumed positions — Kafka's favorite metric.
Reactive Streams
A cross-language spec (Java Flow, RxJS, Project Reactor) that standardizes demand-based backpressure.
High-water mark
The buffer threshold that triggers a backpressure signal.
