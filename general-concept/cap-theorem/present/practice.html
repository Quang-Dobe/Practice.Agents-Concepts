Practice — how it plays out for real

● ~10 min read ✎ best practices · anti-patterns · checklist

Where you'll meet it

Every design review for a multi-region service eventually walks into the CAP question, even when nobody names it: during a partition, do we refuse the request or risk diverging? That one question shapes the database choice, the API contract, the on-call runbook, and the post-mortem template.

Store selection

Picking the database behind a payments ledger, or deciding whether a cart accepts writes from a region that's lost the primary.

Vendor conversations

Arguing with a vendor about what "strongly consistent" actually means on their box.

Incident response

An inter-AZ link flaps for 90 seconds and the dashboards light up.

API design

Deciding, per endpoint, whether a stale read is acceptable or must be refused.

The trap is treating CAP as a one-time static decision ("we're a CP shop"). In real systems the trade-off is made per endpoint, per table, sometimes per request — and the pain comes from endpoints whose consistency contract was never written down.

Best practices

1

Frame every replicated endpoint with an explicit partition contract

Do

Decide, per endpoint, what happens during a partition — serve stale, queue and acknowledge, or refuse with a typed error. Write it in the API doc next to the request schema.

Why

Without this, behavior under partition is whatever the driver's defaults are, which surprises everyone at 3am. A typed 409 PartitionedWrite is a feature; an HTTP 500 followed by a silent retry is an incident.

Avoid

"We'll deal with it when it happens." You will, and badly.

2

Make the consistency level visible at the API boundary

Do

Surface read consistency in the request — e.g. ?consistency=strong on a critical balance read, eventual by default elsewhere. DynamoDB models this with ConsistentRead per request; MongoDB with readConcern: "majority".

Why

Callers downstream make correctness assumptions. If the consistency choice is buried three config layers down, nobody knows whether the balance they just read is authoritative.

Avoid

A single global default that quietly forces every read through the slowest, costliest path — or the cheapest, stalest one.

3

Use leader-based CP for anything that resembles a ledger or a lock

Do

Reach for etcd, ZooKeeper, Spanner, CockroachDB, or a Raft-backed service for leader election, configuration, distributed locks, financial state, and inventory decrement.

Why

These workloads need a single source of truth at write time. "We sold the same seat twice" is not an acceptable eventual-convergence outcome. A majority round-trip is the price of correctness.

Avoid

Hand-rolling a "leader" on top of Redis pub/sub or a Postgres advisory lock and hoping the partition behavior works out. Redlock's correctness has well-documented debates — treat it accordingly.

4

Default to LOCAL_QUORUM for multi-region Dynamo-style stores

Do

In Cassandra, ScyllaDB, or DynamoDB Global Tables, default reads and writes to LOCAL_QUORUM. Reserve cross-region EACH_QUORUM for the few operations that truly need it.

Why

Cross-region quorum buys little on a healthy network and turns one regional brownout into a global outage. Cassandra's cqlsh default is ONE — AP-leaning and usually the wrong choice for application code.

Avoid

Leaving driver defaults in place and discovering on incident day that "quorum" meant "any one replica."

5

Pick conflict resolution on purpose

Do

For AP write paths, decide between last-write-wins, vector clocks, CRDTs conflict-free replicated data types — structures that merge concurrent updates without conflicts, or a domain-specific merge function before shipping. Document which writes can converge by merge and which cannot.

Why

Last-write-wins is a decision, not a default — it silently loses data on concurrent writes, and clock skew makes "last" a fiction. "My cart item vanished" bugs are almost always this.

Avoid

Adopting Cassandra or DynamoDB and assuming concurrent writes "just work." They merge by timestamp — last-write-wins under a friendlier name.

6

Use idempotency keys end-to-end on any AP write path

Do

Every mutating request carries a client-generated idempotency key a client-supplied ID the server uses to recognize and collapse a repeated request; the server stores (key → result) long enough to cover a reasonable retry window (Stripe uses 24h).

Why

AP systems encourage clients to retry on timeout. Without an idempotency key, retries during a partition produce duplicate charges, orders, or emails.

Avoid

Relying on the database's own dedup. It usually doesn't exist at the granularity you need.

7

Design degradation modes, not just success paths

Do

For each user-facing flow, pre-decide one behavior when the store is partitioned: serve stale data with a freshness header, accept into a durable queue and ack "pending," or refuse with a clear error. Test all three.

Why

Partition-state engineering is what separates a 4-nines service from a 2-nines one. Teams that haven't pre-decided default to "5xx everything."

Avoid

Generic retries with exponential backoff as your only partition strategy. They mask the problem until the queue collapses.

8

Test partition behavior on purpose

Do

Run Jepsen-style fault injection, or at minimum tc netem / Toxiproxy in CI, dropping inter-AZ traffic for 30–120 seconds. Verify writes either succeed and stick, fail cleanly, or roll back as documented.

Why

Every major distributed database has been embarrassed by Jepsen at some point. Your application code's behavior under partition is almost certainly worse than the database's.

Avoid

Trusting vendor consistency claims without testing them yourself.

9

Reason in PACELC, not just CAP, for steady-state decisions

Do

When picking between two CP systems or two AP systems, use PACELC's else half — else, latency or consistency? — because partitions are rare and steady-state latency is a daily cost.

Why

CAP's binary buckets group Spanner and ZooKeeper together; PACELC distinguishes them on the dimension that matters at p99 every minute of the day.

Avoid

Picking a strongly-consistent global store for a workload that could tolerate causal consistency, then complaining about the cross-region write latency you signed up for.

10

Match the data to the trade — money to CP, social feeds to AP

Do

Ledgers, inventory, seat reservations, leader election, config → CP. Shopping carts, social feeds, presence, view counters, recommendation state → AP, ideally CRDT-friendly.

Why

A cart that 5xx's during a partition loses revenue and trust; a transient inventory inconsistency that oversells one SKU is a refund and an apology. Pick the failure mode whose cost is lower.

Avoid

The reverse: AP for money (silent overdrafts), CP for newsfeeds (down on every regional blip).

Anti-patterns to recognize

✕

"We're a CA system"

The team decided partitions don't happen on their network. They still happen — the system just degrades in undefined ways. Pick CP or AP explicitly and own the degradation.

✕

Conflating CAP-C with ACID-C

A team argues a single-node Postgres is "CP because ACID." ACID-C means constraints hold; CAP-C means linearizability across replicas. Keep the two vocabularies separate.

✕

"Eventually consistent" used as a synonym for "consistent"

A claim that papers over reads returning arbitrarily stale data. Name the actual model: read-your-writes, monotonic reads, causal, or linearizable.

✕

Read-modify-write on Cassandra without a lightweight transaction

Reading a counter, incrementing in app code, writing back. Concurrent writers silently overwrite each other. Use Cassandra's lightweight transactions (Paxos on the AP path) or a counter type that converges under merge.

✕

Treating CAP as the only design dimension

Ignoring the steady-state latency-vs-consistency trade (PACELC's else-half), which is where most of the operating cost actually lives. Write the consistency contract and the p99 latency target on the same page.

✕

Bolting strong consistency onto an AP system at the app layer

Hand-rolled locks in DynamoDB via conditional writes across multiple items, or "two-phase commit" implemented over an eventual store. These break under partition in subtle ways. Put the strongly-consistent slice on a CP store instead.

✕

Misreading defaults

Assuming DynamoDB reads are strongly consistent (they default to eventual, at half the cost) or that MongoDB writes are durable across the replica set (writeConcern: 1 only waits for the primary). Pin consistency settings explicitly and code-review them.

✕

Trusting wall-clock timestamps for ordering

Last-write-wins with NTP-synchronized clocks looks fine until a leap second or 200ms of skew silently drops a user's write. Use hybrid logical clocks, vector clocks, or a CP store for ordering-sensitive data.

Real-world usage patterns

Multi-region SaaS: CP control plane, AP data plane

Config, tenant metadata, and feature flags on etcd or CockroachDB (CP); the per-tenant event stream on Cassandra or DynamoDB at LOCAL_QUORUM (AP). Lesson: the CP control plane becomes the limiting factor for region failover — Raft leader election time is the real RTO floor, often 5–15 seconds.

Spanner-backed financial ledger

Core ledger on Spanner, using TrueTime for external consistency across regions; bulk analytics use bounded stale reads (read_timestamp = now - 10s) to skip the leader. Lesson: most reads don't need to be linearizable — routing them to stale reads can cut p99 latency by an order of magnitude.

Collaborative editor with CRDTs

A Figma-style document editor uses CRDTs (Yjs or Automerge) for the document body and a CP store (Postgres, strict serializable) for permissions and billing. Lesson: even in a "fully local-first" product, the boundary between CRDT-land and CP-land is where most bugs live — permission revocation racing an in-flight edit is the canonical example.

E-commerce: Cassandra plus lightweight transactions

Cart contents and catalog in Cassandra at LOCAL_QUORUM (fast, AP-leaning); inventory decrement at checkout uses Cassandra's Paxos-backed lightweight transactions. Lesson: lightweight transactions run at roughly 4x the latency of a normal write — using them everywhere defeats the point of Cassandra, using them nowhere oversells stock during a flash sale.

Kafka as a durable buffer in front of an AP store

A telemetry pipeline writes with acks=all and min.insync.replicas=2 (CP-like durability), then fans out into Cassandra. Lesson: unclean.leader.election.enable=true silently flips this from CP to AP and can lose committed offsets — verify it's off in production.

Operational checklist

✓

Before you call it done

  • Monitoring: do you alert on partition signatures — inter-AZ packet loss, leader election rate, replica lag, hinted-handoff queue depth?
  • Failure handling: for each replicated endpoint, is the documented partition behavior actually tested with fault injection in CI?
  • Idempotency: does every mutating endpoint accept a client-generated idempotency key, with a dedup window long enough for your retry strategy?
  • Consistency defaults: are the client's consistency / read-concern / write-concern settings pinned explicitly in code, not left to driver defaults?
  • Split-brain protection: for CP systems, is N odd, are leases longer than your worst-case GC pause, and is unclean leader election off?
  • Conflict resolution: for AP writes, is the merge strategy documented per data type, avoiding silent last-write-wins where it matters?
  • Cost: are you paying for strong reads or cross-region quorum on endpoints that don't need them?
  • Security foot-gun: is authorization data — a revoked permission — read on a strong-read code path, not a stale one?
  • Runbook: does the on-call runbook say what to do if the store reports "no quorum" — fail over, wait, or page someone?
  • Onboarding: can a new engineer answer "what happens to a write to endpoint X if us-east-1 is isolated?" on day one?

How it evolves in a codebase

  1. Single region, no CAP thinking. A managed Postgres with read replicas. The first CAP-shaped bug appears when app code reads from a lagging replica and gets data the user just wrote — fixed with sticky sessions or route-primary-after-write, often without naming it.
  2. Multi-region forces a real choice. Either run a CP global store (Spanner, CockroachDB) and pay the write-latency tax, or shard data by region and accept eventual cross-region reads. Most teams pick sharding because it's cheaper, then spend two years handling the cases where data crosses regional boundaries.
  3. Mature hybrid state. A small CP core (identity, billing, leader election) on a strongly-consistent store; a large AP body (events, content, social state) on a Dynamo-style store with documented per-endpoint contracts; a thin CRDT or merge layer for offline-tolerant collaboration. Teams that reach this stage wish they'd written the partition contract down on day one.

Further reading

  • Please stop calling databases CP or AP — Martin Kleppmann — why the static labels mislead, from the author of Designing Data-Intensive Applications.
  • Jepsen analyses — Kyle Kingsbury's empirical takedowns of distributed databases under partition.
  • Inside Cloud Spanner and the CAP Theorem — Eric Brewer — Brewer himself on why Spanner is "effectively CA" in practice, with honest caveats.
  • Consistency Tradeoffs in Modern Distributed Database System Design (PACELC) — Daniel Abadi — the paper that adds the missing latency-vs-consistency axis CAP omits.
  • Designing Data-Intensive Applications — Martin Kleppmann — Chapters 5 (Replication) and 9 (Consistency and Consensus) are the canonical practitioner's reference.
  • A Critique of the CAP Theorem — Martin Kleppmann — the case that CAP is too coarse to be a design tool on its own, and what to use instead.
